Motions of the Plane

Translation, rotation, reflection, and glide reflection — every motion of the plane falls into one of three classes. The three-nails theorem explains why, and Chasles' theorem gives the full classification. Below: interactive models instead of long proofs.

1. What a motion preserves

A motion of the plane is a transformation that preserves distances: if the distance between points $A$ and $B$ was $x$, then after the motion the distance between their images $A'$ and $B'$ is still $x$ — for every pair of points.

The simplest example is a translation: every point shifts by the same vector, so the distances between them don't change. It will turn out that there really aren't many "simple" kinds of plane motion — and together they exhaust the whole list.

3. The three-nails theorem

If a motion leaves three points $A$, $B$, $C$ fixed and they don't lie on one line, it leaves absolutely everything fixed — that is, it's the identity ($\mathrm{id}$). Drag $A$, $B$, $C$, and a test point $P$: the three circles of radii $|AP|$, $|BP|$, $|CP|$ around the nails meet at exactly one point — $P$ itself. So no other point can be the image of $P$ under a motion that fixes $A,B,C$.

Degenerate case $C$ has landed on line $AB$: what's fixed is no longer a pair of points but the whole line $AB$ — and everything off it is no longer fixed. One degree of freedom is left — reflection about $AB$.

Since three fixed points off one line always mean $\mathrm{id}$, let's work through the more modest cases in decreasing order.

Two fixed points. This isn't a coincidence from the degenerate case above but a general fact — the two-point lemma: if a motion leaves distinct points $A$ and $B$ fixed, it leaves the whole line $AB$ fixed — for a point $C$ on that line the equality of lengths ($|AC|+|CB|=|AB|$) must be preserved, while for a point off the line it would turn into a strict inequality. And there can't be a third fixed point off that line either: otherwise, by the theorem above, everything would be fixed. So if a motion isn't the identity but at least a pair of points is fixed, exactly the whole line through them is fixed and nothing more — that's a reflection about it.

One fixed point. The motion can't fix a second one — by the same lemma, that would fix an entire line. This is a rotation about it — you already saw this in §2.

No fixed points at all. We already have one example — a nonzero translation. But, as it turns out next, that's not the only answer: there's one more, fourth, kind of motion.

4. Glide reflection

The composition of a reflection and a shift along its own axis is also a motion with no fixed point whatsoever, yet it can't be reduced to a translation, a rotation, or a plain reflection. This is the promised fourth, independent kind — glide reflection.

The composition of two glide reflections is a translation if their axes are parallel, and a rotation (by twice the angle between the axes) if the axes cross — exactly as for two plain reflections: the glide (shift) component of both cancels out along the way. The same effect shows up for rotation∘rotation: a translation if the sum of the angles is a multiple of $2\pi$, otherwise a rotation by that sum.

6. Chasles' theorem

Theorem (Chasles)

Every motion of the plane belongs to exactly one of three classes:

$\rightrightarrows$ translation (including the zero one — the identity motion);

$\circlearrowleft$ rotation about an arbitrary center;

$\leftharpoonup\leftharpoondown$ glide reflection — including a plain reflection as the case of zero shift.

The identity motion is a translation by the zero vector; a plain reflection is a glide reflection with zero shift along the axis. No other motions of the plane exist.

Proof (for the curious)

Let's sort motions by their number of fixed points. At each step, if there aren't enough fixed points for an already-handled case, we multiply the motion by a suitable "corrective" motion — so the number of fixed points goes up by one — and reduce to the previous case. Any extra branch that would contradict the number of fixed points of the original motion gets discarded using the composition table (§5).

Three or more fixed points. This is the three-nails theorem (§3) — the motion is the identity.

Exactly two. Handled there too (the two-point lemma): the whole line $AB$ is fixed and nothing more — this is the reflection $S_{AB}$.

Exactly one, $O$. Take any $P\ne O$ and set $P'=G(P)$. Since $|OP|=|OP'|$, the points $P$ and $P'$ lie on one circle centered at $O$; let $R$ be the rotation about $O$ sending $P'$ to $P$. Then $R\circ G$ fixes both $O$ and $P$ — already two fixed points, so by the previous case $R\circ G=\mathrm{id}$ or $R\circ G=S_{OP}$. The second option is impossible: by the composition table, rotation∘reflection sharing the point $O$ is again a reflection through $O$, which fixes an entire line rather than one point — contradicting the hypothesis. That leaves $G=R^{-1}$ — a rotation about $O$.

None at all. Take any point $O$, set $A=G(O)\ne O$, and let $T$ be the translation sending $A$ back to $O$. Then $T\circ G$ fixes $O$, so by the cases already handled it's $\mathrm{id}$, a rotation about $O$, or a reflection about some line $m\ni O$. The "rotation" branch is impossible: by the table, translation∘rotation is again a rotation, and a rotation always has a fixed point — its center — contradicting the hypothesis that $G$ has none. The "$\mathrm{id}$" branch gives $G=T^{-1}$ — a translation (nonzero, since $A\ne O$). The "reflection $S_m$" branch gives $G=T^{-1}\circ S_m$: by the table, translation∘reflection is again a reflection (if the translation runs along $m$ — but then a whole line is fixed again, a contradiction) or a glide reflection with axis parallel to $m$ and nonzero shift along it — only this outcome leaves no fixed points.

All four cases exhaust the possibilities — and that's Chasles' theorem.

7. Reflection as a "prime" motion

Between Chasles' theorem and the composition table there's a stronger fact: reflection isn't just one of the four classes — it's the "building block" for all the others.

Theorem

Every motion of the plane is a composition of at most three axial reflections: $\mathrm{id}$ is the empty composition (0 reflections); translation and rotation take exactly two; reflection takes one; glide reflection takes three.

This is exactly the fact about rotation∘reflection and translation∘reflection that we already cited in the proof of Chasles' theorem above (§6) — here's what it looks like in pictures. Reflect about $l_1$, then about $l_2$: if the axes meet at a point $O$ at angle $\theta$ (from $l_1$ to $l_2$) — the result is a rotation about $O$ by $2\theta$; if the axes are parallel with distance $d$ between them — the result is a translation by $2d$ perpendicular to the axes, from $l_1$ to $l_2$.

Adding a third reflection with any axis can never again give an orientation-preserving motion: by the composition table (§5), the parity of the number of reflections determines the kind of the result — an odd count always gives a reflection or a glide reflection.

8. Motions as functions $f(z)$

Treating the plane as the field of complex numbers, every motion is written as a single formula. Adding a number is a translation; multiplying by a number with $|a|=1$ is a rotation about zero; conjugation $\bar z$ is a reflection about the real axis. Putting these operations together gives the whole family:

$f(z) = az + b$, $|a|=1$ — orientation-preserving motions (translation, rotation): $a=1$ — a translation by vector $b$; $a\ne1$ — a rotation by angle $\arg a$ about the point $z_0=b/(1-a)$.

$f(z) = a\bar z + b$, $|a|=1$ — orientation-reversing motions (reflection, glide reflection).

What if we drop the restriction $|a|=1$? Then the coefficient $a$ is no longer a pure rotation/conjugation — a stretch appears, and we enter the world of similarities. Covered in a separate material, "Similarities of the Plane: Homothety and Rotational Homothety".

9. Recovering the formula from images of points

If a motion is given not by the geometric parameters of its axes and vectors but by the images of specific points, its complex formula can be recovered algebraically. Two or three points are enough.

If the parity of the motion is known

When it's already known whether the motion preserves orientation or reverses it, two distinct points $z_1, z_2$ and their images $w_1, w_2$ are enough to pin it down uniquely.

For an orientation-preserving motion ($w = az + b$) the coefficients come from a linear system:

$$a = \frac{w_2 - w_1}{z_2 - z_1}, \quad b = \frac{w_1 z_2 - w_2 z_1}{z_2 - z_1}$$

For an orientation-reversing motion ($w = a\bar{z} + b$) conjugation shows up in the denominators:

$$a = \frac{w_2 - w_1}{\bar{z}_2 - \bar{z}_1}, \quad b = \frac{w_1 \bar{z}_2 - w_2 \bar{z}_1}{\bar{z}_2 - \bar{z}_1}$$

If the parity of the motion is unknown

Two points aren't enough in this case: they leave an ambiguity. There is exactly one orientation-preserving and exactly one orientation-reversing motion sending the directed segment $z_1z_2$ to $w_1w_2$. A third point $z_3$, not on the line through the first two, is needed, along with its image $w_3$.

The check is simple: from $z_1, z_2$ compute the coefficients $a, b$ assuming the motion is orientation-preserving. If the equality $w_3 = a z_3 + b$ holds for the third point, the guess is confirmed. If the identity doesn't hold, the motion is guaranteed to be orientation-reversing, and the coefficients need to be recomputed with the formulas for $\bar{z}$.
